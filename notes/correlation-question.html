Which correlation matrix should you trust? An open question for portfolio builders

Samuel Edet · October 2026 · from ongoing research with Lionel Yelibi

Almost every portfolio method used by institutional investors runs on an estimated correlation matrix. That estimate is noisy, and optimisers make the noise worse. There are two broad ways to clean it, but no one has shown fairly when each one should be used. We think the answer depends on how a market's assets are connected, and that this can be measured.

The problem: optimisers chase estimation error

Minimum variance, risk parity, maximum diversification and hierarchical risk parity all turn a correlation matrix into portfolio weights. With 500 stocks and a year of daily data, there are more than 120,000 correlations to estimate from about 250 observations per stock. Many of those estimates are mostly noise.

An optimiser does not treat that noise neutrally. It looks for combinations of assets that appear to cancel each other's risk, and those are exactly the combinations the data measure worst. Richard Michaud called this "error maximisation" in 1989. The result is weights that swing from one rebalance to the next even when nothing in the market has changed.

This is why simple rules are so hard to beat. An equal-weighted portfolio often does as well out of sample as an optimised one (DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal, 2009). And imposing constraints that are, in theory, wrong, such as banning short sales, often makes portfolios better (Jagannathan and Ma, 2003). Both results reflect the same underlying problem.

Two families of fixes

  • Shrinkage and spectral cleaning. Methods such as Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage and random-matrix eigenvalue clipping pull extreme estimates toward more sensible values. They assume nothing about which assets are linked. They are cheap, robust and widely used.
  • Network filtering. Methods such as the graphical lasso and filtered correlation networks (for example TMFG) keep only the strongest direct links between assets and set the rest to zero. They make a strong assumption that most pairs of assets are not directly connected, and in return they reveal structure, such as clusters of closely linked stocks.

The two families are rarely tested against each other on equal terms. Network methods are often benchmarked against the raw, uncleaned matrix, which is an easy target, rather than against the best shrinkage methods. So practitioners have little guidance on which to use.

Our starting point: it depends where the structure lives

Asset returns move together for two different reasons:

  • Shared exposure to common factors. Chevron and ConocoPhillips move together not because either affects the other, but because both depend on the oil price. The market as a whole, sectors and styles work the same way.
  • Direct links between particular assets. Some pairs are connected in ways that do not run through any broad factor, for example through supply chains, shared customers or substitution.

We expect shrinkage to do well when factors dominate, and network filters to do well when direct links dominate. Real markets are a mix, and we expect sector-like structure to be the hardest case for network filters. If that is right, the useful question is not which method is better, but which kind of market you are in.

Why it matters for investors

  • Choosing tools: asset owners and managers using minimum-variance, risk-parity or diversification strategies need to know which cleaning method suits their asset universe, not just which one did best in someone else's backtest.
  • Hidden costs: a poorly matched method may not show up as higher measured risk. It can show up instead as unstable weights, higher turnover and higher trading costs.
  • Reading market structure: knowing how much of a market's co-movement is driven by broad factors and how much by direct links is useful in its own right, for risk management and for diversification.

This note describes a research question, not results. The study is under way, and we will report what we find, including if it shows that one family of methods wins everywhere.
