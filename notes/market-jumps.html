Not all crashes are alike: jumps and volatility in developed and emerging stock markets

Samuel Edet · October 2026 · based on daily index data for 11 markets, 2005–2025

Standard risk models assume that daily stock returns follow a bell curve. They do not. In 11 major markets, the normal model understates one-day tail risk by up to 45%. Most of the extra risk comes from volatility arriving in waves, but sudden jumps matter too. In several large emerging markets, jumps come less often but hit harder.

The question

Many tools in finance, from the Black–Scholes option formula to simple Value-at-Risk models, assume that returns are normally distributed with a constant volatility. In practice, markets have more very bad and very good days than a bell curve allows. There are two classic explanations. Either prices sometimes jump, on news, crises or policy surprises, or volatility shifts, so that calm and turbulent periods come in runs. The two have different implications for risk management, and they may differ between developed and emerging markets.

This note fits five models to daily returns of the main stock index in five developed markets (United States, Japan, United Kingdom, Germany, Australia) and six emerging markets (Brazil, Mexico, India, China, South Africa, Indonesia) from 2005 to 2025: the normal benchmark, two jump-diffusion models (Merton and Kou), a GARCH model of volatility clustering, and a GARCH model with jumps.

The normal model misses the tails everywhere

Daily returns in every market have far fatter tails than a normal distribution. A risk manager who used the normal model to set the 1% one-day Value at Risk, the loss exceeded on one day in a hundred, would have set it too low in all 11 markets.

Waves of volatility matter more than jumps

When markets get turbulent, they tend to stay turbulent for weeks. A GARCH model, which lets volatility rise after large moves and decay slowly, fits the data better than either jump-diffusion model in all 11 markets, even though it has fewer parameters. Models with constant volatility "explain" fat tails partly by mistaking turbulent periods for jumps: fitted that way, the data imply 70 to 280 jumps a year, which is not a plausible description of how markets move.

But jumps are real, and their character differs

Allowing for jumps on top of volatility clustering improves the fit in every market, and the combined model is the best of the five everywhere. Once clustering is accounted for, jumps make up a similar share of risk across markets: 11–29% of return variance in developed and emerging markets alike. The average jump is negative everywhere: jumps are mostly falls.

What differs is the kind of jump. In the US, UK and Germany, jumps are frequent and small. In China, Brazil, India and Indonesia, they are rarer but much larger, so large discontinuous moves happen several times a year even after allowing for turbulent periods.

"Emerging" is not one category. Mexico and South Africa, with deep and internationally integrated markets, look like developed markets. The analysis does not identify why, but a plausible explanation is that policy interventions, capital-flow reversals and narrower investor bases move prices more abruptly in the markets with large jumps.

What it means

  • Risk managers: normal Value at Risk is too low everywhere, by about a quarter in a typical market. Models with time-varying volatility should be the default. For markets such as China, Brazil, India and Indonesia, add explicit jump or stress scenarios, because volatility models alone respond only after the first large move.
  • Portfolio construction: volatility is not the whole story when comparing markets. Two markets with similar volatility can carry very different crash risk. Allocations to markets with large jumps deserve a larger tail-risk budget, and position limits that do not rely on recent calm.
  • Option pricing and hedging: constant-volatility jump models overstate how often markets jump. Hedges and option prices should combine stochastic volatility with a smaller number of larger jumps.
  • Development finance and policymakers: frequent large jumps are a sign of market fragility. Deeper capital markets, with broader investor bases and predictable policy communication, are likely to reduce them, though this analysis does not test that directly.

Jumps are inferred statistically from daily closing prices, not identified from news, and some of what the models call jumps are better read as fat-tailed shocks. Parameters are assumed constant over 21 years, although jump risk varies with market conditions. Index levels are in local currency and exclude dividends. GARCH shocks are assumed normal; heavier-tailed shocks would attribute some jump risk to the shocks themselves.

About the analysis

  • Data: daily closing levels of the S&P 500, Nikkei 225, FTSE 100, DAX, S&P/ASX 200, Ibovespa, IPC, BSE Sensex, SSE Composite, FTSE/JSE Top 40 and Jakarta Composite, January 2005 to December 2025, from Yahoo Finance.
  • Models: geometric Brownian motion; Merton (1976) and Kou (2002) jump-diffusions, with the exact Kou density computed by Fourier inversion; GARCH(1,1) (Bollerslev, 1986); and GARCH(1,1) with Poisson normal jumps. All estimated by maximum likelihood, with parameter-recovery checks on simulated data.
  • Code: github.com/samueledet/market-jumps-volatility
