The hurdles and high costs of digital adoption in Africa

Marcio Cruz, Samuel Edet and Maty Konte · first published by IFC Economics, 11 February 2025

Digital technology can raise firms' productivity, but in Africa it costs more to buy, more to run and more to import. Cost is not the whole story, but it is a large part of why adoption is low.

  • >20%of income per person: average cost of fixed broadband in Sub-Saharan Africa
  • <6%the same cost in North Africa, South Asia, and Latin America and the Caribbean
  • 35–39%higher machinery and equipment prices in Sub-Saharan Africa than in the US
  • ~3×tariffs on digital goods in Africa compared with the rest of the world

Five hurdles

  1. Digital goods cost more

    Machinery, equipment and software cost much more in Africa than in other regions. Relative to the United States, machinery and equipment cost 35–39% more in Sub-Saharan Africa and 13–15% more in North Africa.

  2. Connectivity is expensive

    On average, fixed broadband in Sub-Saharan Africa costs more than a fifth of income per person. In North Africa, South Asia, and Latin America and the Caribbean it costs less than 6%.

  3. Power is unreliable

    Where electricity is scarce or erratic, internet use is low too. Firms are unlikely to invest in digital tools they cannot keep switched on.

  4. Importing is slow and costly

    Tariffs on digital goods are about three times the rest of the world's average. Weak infrastructure and logistics add time and cost to every shipment.

  5. Markets lack competition

    Monopolies and state-owned enterprises dominate some key sectors. This shuts out smaller firms and weakens the incentive to invest and innovate.

What would help

  • Off-grid and mini-grid renewable power to make electricity reliable where the grid falls short
  • Lower trade barriers and deeper regional market integration to cut the cost of digital goods
  • Competition policy that opens key sectors to new and smaller firms
  • Digital skills training and better access to finance for small and medium-sized enterprises

Cost is not the only barrier. Skills and firm capabilities also explain why African firms use the digital tools they already have less intensively.
