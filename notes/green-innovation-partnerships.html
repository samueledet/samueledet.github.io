Partners at the frontier: how collaboration lifts green innovation in developing economies

Samuel Edet · November 2026 · based on CEPR Discussion Paper 22135, with Ralf Martin, Golvine de Rochambeau, Trang Thu Tran and Dennis Verhoeven

Green technology is a field where developing countries already innovate well. Their most valuable green inventions come from partnerships with researchers in rich countries, yet the world's biggest R&D firms do very little of this work. Doing more would raise the returns to innovation in both rich and developing economies.

  • 48%of developing-country green innovations are co-invented with partners in high-income countries
  • 2%of high-income-country innovations involve a developing-country inventor
  • 4×the knowledge spillovers when a developing-country green innovation has a rich-country partner
  • ~40%share of a firm's innovations with developing-country partners at which spillovers peak, against 2% today

The question

Growth success stories are often tied to new technology waves. China built strong positions not only in making solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles, but also in the research behind them. Do other emerging and developing economies have a similar opening in green technologies? And if green R&D there is valuable, who will pay for it, given tight public budgets?

The paper uses about 20 million inventions (patent families) filed worldwide in 2013–2022, from the European Patent Office's PATSTAT database. An invention counts as green if it is classified under climate-change mitigation or adaptation technologies. Its value to others is measured by the knowledge spillovers it generates: the value of the later inventions that build on it, directly or indirectly, traced through the patent citation network. Throughout, "developing economies" excludes China, which is a story of its own.

Green innovation in developing economies is high quality

Inventions from developing economies generate smaller spillovers than those from high-income countries on average, but the gap is much smaller for green technology. A green invention from a developing economy generates about $8.0 million of spillovers, more than the average high-income-country invention across all technologies ($7.7 million).

Allowing for the cost of producing new inventions in each field makes the case stronger. The estimated marginal return to further R&D in developing economies rises from 61% across all technologies to 93% for green technologies, a bigger jump than in high-income countries (85% to 107%). Inventions from developing economies also generate particularly large spillovers to other developing economies, because knowledge travels most easily between similar places.

The most valuable inventions come from partnerships

Innovation in developing economies is unusually collaborative. Almost half of their green inventions (48%) involve at least one inventor in a high-income country. By contrast, about 90% of high-income-country inventions are made entirely within high-income countries, and only 2% involve a developing-country inventor.

These partnerships produce the most valuable inventions. A developing-country green invention with a rich-country partner generates about four times the spillovers of one made without any foreign partner.

The value does not simply flow out to rich countries. Partnered green inventions also generate three times the spillovers to other developing-country innovators ($0.87 million against $0.28 million). That suggests developing economies have the capacity to absorb and build on what these partnerships produce.

Top R&D firms are doing too little of it

The world's largest R&D investors, the firms in the EU's Industrial R&D Investment Scoreboard, are the natural partners. About 4,400 of them produce nearly half of all collaborations between high-income and developing-economy inventors. Yet the typical firm has no invention with a developing-country inventor at all, and on average only about 2% of a firm's inventions involve one.

Comparing firms shows that this is far too little. Spillovers rise with the share of a firm's inventions made with developing-country partners, peak, and then fall: an inverted U. Spillovers to developing economies peak when about 44% of a firm's inventions involve developing-country partners, and spillovers to high-income countries when about 39% do. Firms that work only in developing economies do worse than diversified firms, which keep a foot in both.

The pattern holds after accounting for firm size, R&D spending, the cost of innovation and the private value of a firm's inventions, and it is the same for green technologies. One explanation is diversity: teams that combine knowledge from different places reach more complementary ideas, so their inventions feed into more later inventions, in both rich and developing countries.

Why firms will not do it on their own

The firms themselves do not gain. The private value of a firm's inventions shows no such inverted U: as far as their own returns go, firms appear to be diversified about as much as suits them. The gains from more partnerships accrue to others, in rich and developing countries alike. That gap between private and social returns is the classic case for public support.

What it means

  • High-income-country governments: most of the extra spillovers from more diversified R&D accrue in high-income countries themselves. That gives a self-interested case for tax credits for firms that do R&D with partners in developing economies, at a time when aid budgets are being cut.
  • Development banks: co-investing in R&D partnerships between multinational firms and developing-country innovators can raise the returns to green innovation where public budgets are tight, and may draw in private finance.
  • Developing-economy policymakers: green technology is a field where local innovation is already of high quality. Making it easier for foreign firms to set up research labs and work with local inventors helps capture that potential.

Patents capture only part of innovation, and spillovers measured through citations miss other channels, such as a local firm simply adopting a technology. The estimated peak in diversification is not a precise optimum: moving from 2% to 40% would be a large change with effects beyond those studied. The firm-level comparisons are associations, robust to many controls, rather than the result of an experiment.
