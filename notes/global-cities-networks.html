The shift east: how global cities' innovation networks changed, 2000–2014

Samuel Edet · February 2022 · based on research with René Belderbos, Florence Benoit, Geon Ho Lee and Massimo Riccaboni

A small group of large cities produces a big share of the world's inventions, and more and more of that work is done with partners abroad. Between 2000 and 2014, cross-border co-invention grew strongly. Asian cities moved up fastest, led by Shanghai, Bangalore and Beijing.

  • 125global cities in 46 countries, tracked through 7 million geo-coded patents
  • >40%of the world's patented inventions come from these cities, home to about 15% of its people
  • +55%growth in the average city's foreign co-invention links, 2000–04 to 2010–14
  • 7×growth in Shanghai's foreign links, from 26th to 2nd place in the world

Why look at cities' innovation networks

Studies of global cities usually measure their connections through airline routes or the international offices of banks, law firms and consultancies. Much less is known about how these cities are linked through innovation. Yet access to knowledge from elsewhere is what keeps a city's inventors from going stale, and it helps them combine ideas in new ways.

This research measures those links directly. It geo-codes the home addresses of inventors on 16 million patents filed between 2000 and 2014. A link is counted whenever inventors in two different countries are named on the same patent and at least one of them lives in one of 125 global cities.

Cross-border co-invention is growing

The average global city's number of foreign co-invention links rose by 55% between 2000–04 and 2010–14. After a dip following the 2008 financial crisis, growth accelerated sharply. Cities are also relying more on foreign partners relative to their own patent output.

Who rose and who fell

US cities still lead. San Francisco overtook New York as the most internationally connected innovation city, and Boston and Philadelphia also grew. But Asian cities climbed fastest. Shanghai's foreign links grew about sevenfold. Bangalore, Beijing and Shenzhen all entered the world's top 40. Europe's leading cities, such as Paris, London, Munich and Brussels, held roughly steady, and some, along with Tokyo and Detroit, lost ground.

Asia is building its own networks too

Even as Asian cities added foreign partners, a smaller share of their collaboration was international: foreign links fell from 70% to 56% of all their co-invention links. The reason is that several Chinese cities became innovation hubs at the same time, and inventors increasingly worked with partners in other cities at home. Asia is connecting outward and inward at once.

Smaller cities depend most on foreign partners

Measured by foreign links per patent, the most internationally dependent cities are smaller or more peripheral ones: Penang, Kiev, Bangkok, Jakarta and Bangalore top the list, and no US city appears in the top 40. In countries where innovation is concentrated in one main city, such as Thailand, there are few domestic partners to work with, so collaboration has to cross borders.

What it means

  • Firms and investors: the map of where R&D partners can be found shifted toward Asia well before the current debate on technology and supply chains. Cities such as Shanghai, Bangalore and Shenzhen became core nodes of the global innovation network, not just production sites.
  • Smaller and emerging-market cities: international links are their main route into global innovation. Policies that make cross-border collaboration easier, such as researcher mobility, joint research programmes and openness to multinational R&D, matter most for them. This fits the finding in how cities enter new technologies that outside ties matter most for non-global cities.
  • Established hubs: Europe's leading cities kept their connections but did not grow them much. Staying central in the network cannot be taken for granted.

The data end in 2014, so this is a picture of the long-run shift that shaped today's landscape, not a current ranking. Much has changed since, including US–China technology tensions and the spread of remote collaboration. Network measures also partly reflect city size: cities that patent more will tend to have more links. About 11% of patents with inventor country data could not be geo-coded.

  • Data: inventor addresses from the European Patent Office's PATSTAT database, geo-coded and grouped into patent families. Cities are defined as functional urban areas (OECD method), using population density and commuting flows.
  • Cities: global cities from the Globalization and World Cities (GaWC) ranking and other city indices, with at least one million people and 50 patents a year.
