Build on what you know, connect to who knows: how cities enter new technologies

Samuel Edet · August 2023 · based on research with René Belderbos and Massimo Riccaboni

Cities rarely leap into technologies far from what they already do. They move into neighbouring fields, and they get there faster when they are connected to inventors elsewhere who already work in those fields. Entering together with partners in other cities pays off afterwards.

  • 1,220cities on six continents, tracked across 646 technology classes, 2005–2014
  • 103,049entries by a city into a technology class that was new to it
  • +73%more citations to a city's later patents in the field when it entered with partners elsewhere
  • +8%higher likelihood of entry per standard deviation of outside ties, for non-global cities

The question

To grow and stay competitive, cities need to develop skills in technologies that are new to them. Where does that capacity come from? There are two possible sources. A city can look inward and build on the know-how it already has. Or it can look outward and learn from inventors in other cities who already work in the new field. Most earlier studies looked at one source at a time, and mostly at regions in Europe or the OECD.

This research looks at both together, at the level of cities, and worldwide. It uses a new geo-located patent database covering 1,220 cities in North America, Europe, Asia, South America, Oceania and Africa, and follows when each city first starts inventing in each of 646 technology classes.

Route 1: build on what you know

A city is more likely to enter a technology that is closely related to what it already patents. This holds in every region and for both global and non-global cities. The further a new field is from a city's core technology, the less likely it is to enter. If it does enter, its patents in that field also perform worse.

How strongly relatedness matters differs a lot by region. Asian cities use their existing know-how to move into neighbouring fields fastest. European cities move much more slowly.

Route 2: connect to who knows

Ties to inventors in other cities who have already worked in a field raise the chance that a city enters it. On average across all cities, the effect is small. It becomes large in two situations:

  • When partner cities are big innovation hubs. Ties help most when they lead to cities with a large pool of inventors. Those ties connect the city to global flows of knowledge, not just to one partner.
  • When the city is not a global hub itself. Global cities attract talent and know-how anyway, so for them relatedness is what counts. For non-global cities, outside ties matter more than relatedness.

Entering together pays off

Just under half of all entries (47.7%) involved inventors from more than one city. Those collaborative entries performed better afterwards: patents the city filed in the new field over the following three years received about 73% more citations than after a solo entry. Cities with a larger share of patents owned by foreign firms also did better after entry (+8% per standard deviation), consistent with multinationals bringing know-how with them.

This is an association, not proof of cause. Stronger teams and more promising projects may be more likely to collaborate in the first place. But it fits the main finding: outside connections help cities learn new fields.

What it means

  • City and regional policymakers: diversify into fields next to existing strengths rather than chasing distant "hot" technologies. This is the logic behind the EU's smart specialisation strategy and Canada's Global Innovation Clusters.
  • Smaller and emerging-market cities: they gain most from links outside. Programmes that fund collaboration with inventors in large hubs, such as joint research grants, diaspora networks and mobility schemes, can help them reach fields their own base cannot.
  • Investors and development finance: a city's existing technology mix and its connections to other cities are a useful guide to which new sectors it can realistically grow. Multinational presence helps new fields perform.

The evidence covers 2005–2014 and is based on patents, which miss innovation that is never patented. The estimates are associations from hazard and count models with fixed effects, not causal effects. City size is measured by number of inventors, which can be affected by inconsistent spelling of inventor names.

About the research

  • Belderbos, R., S. Edet and M. Riccaboni (2023). "Exploratory Innovation in Cities: Inter-city Ties and Technological Relatedness." SSRN working paper. ssrn.com/abstract=4557519
  • Method: survival (hazard) models of a city's first entry into each technology class; negative binomial models of citations after entry, with city, technology and year fixed effects.
  • Data: a geo-referenced patent database covering 1,220 cities and 646 four-digit IPC classes, 2005–2014.
