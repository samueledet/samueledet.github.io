Is it the invention or the inventor? Why emerging-market patents lose value early

Samuel Edet · October 2026 · early results from ongoing research

Patents from emerging markets are dropped by their owners sooner than patents from rich economies. Is that because the inventions are worth less, or because their owners cannot afford to turn them into products? Early evidence from 3.1 million patents suggests both: technology explains most of the gap, but for individuals and small firms, the owner looks like the binding constraint, not the invention.

The question

A patent is an option: the right, for up to 20 years, to turn an invention into a product without being copied. Owners pay a renewal fee every year to keep it, and they stop paying when the patent is no longer worth the fee. Renewals therefore reveal value, for private firms and individuals as well as listed companies.

If emerging-market patents are dropped early, there are two possible reasons. The underlying technologies may simply be less valuable. Or good inventions may never realise their value because their owners lack the finance, partners or markets to commercialise them. The two call for different policies: research and skills in the first case, finance and technology markets in the second.

How the test works

The analysis uses PATSTAT, the worldwide patent database of the European Patent Office, and follows every granted patent filed in 2000–2005 at the patent offices of China, Korea and Brazil, and of the United States, Japan, Germany and the United Kingdom for comparison: 3.1 million patents, each observed for close to 20 years. The outcome is whether the owner still renews the patent ten years after filing.

Comparisons are made within the same patent office, where everyone pays the same fees under the same law: domestic owners against foreign owners. The question is whether domestic owners in emerging-market offices fall further behind than domestic owners in rich-economy offices. Technology quality is held fixed using forward citations, technology field and owner type.

Most of the gap is the technology

Before any adjustment, the gap is large. In China, domestic owners are 25 percentage points more likely than foreign owners to have dropped a patent by year ten. But foreign-owned patents are a selected group: inventions judged valuable enough to protect abroad, with four to seven times the citations. Once citations, technology field and owner type are accounted for, about three quarters of the extra emerging-market gap disappears. Among firms with more than 100 inventions, no significant gap remains.

What remains sits with small owners

The remaining gap is concentrated among individual inventors and small firms. At equal observable quality, they let their patents lapse far more often than comparable small owners in rich economies. The same pattern holds in China, Korea and Brazil separately, and at the US patent office for owners from Taiwan, where fees, law and enforcement are identical for everyone.

China's provincial subsidies for filing patents are an obvious suspect, since they encouraged patents that were cheap to abandon. But the gap for Chinese individuals and small firms did not grow as the subsidies spread, and Korea, without comparable subsidies, shows the same pattern. Chinese universities are the exception: their large gap grew over time and looks like patenting to meet evaluation targets.

A new owner changes the outcome

The sharpest test looks at what happens when a patent changes hands. Buyers choose valuable patents everywhere, so a sold patent is expected to outlive one that is not sold. The question is whether a sale helps more when the seller is a small emerging-market owner. It does, by a wide margin. A patent sold by a small owner in China or Korea is 24 points more likely to still be renewed five years later than a comparable unsold one; for small owners in rich economies the gain is 5 points. In China, patents sold to companies survive 10 points longer than those sold to individuals.

The same inventions are worth keeping in other hands. That points to the owner's capacity, to finance, produce and sell, as the constraint, rather than the quality of the idea. Two further checks narrow this down. When small owners patent the same invention at home and abroad, they keep the home patent at least as long as the foreign one, so weak protection or a difficult home market is not the problem. And their patents were not dropped more often during credit crunches in China, Korea and Brazil, so short-term lack of cash is not the problem either. What remains is a persistent gap in what small owners can do with an invention.

What it means, if it holds

  • Development finance and investors: small innovators in emerging markets may hold more valuable technology than their renewal behaviour suggests. Short-term credit is unlikely to be the fix: their patents were not dropped more often when credit tightened. What helps is longer-term capacity to produce and sell, through equity, partnerships with larger firms, or selling and licensing the technology to owners who can use it.
  • Policymakers: markets for technology matter. Making it easier to sell or license patents, through registries, valuation services and enforcement, lets inventions move to owners who can use them. Subsidies for filing patents, by contrast, add patents that are quickly dropped.
  • Large firms: for them the evidence points to technology, not frictions. Their gap is about what they invent, which research and skills policies address.

These are early, descriptive results. Renewal is a proxy for value: turning survival into monetary values requires each office's fee schedule and a structural model of renewal decisions, which is the next step. Citations do not capture every aspect of quality, so part of the small-owner gap may reflect quality that is not measured, although the sale results speak against that being the whole story. India, Mexico and South Africa cannot yet be studied this way because their renewal records are not in PATSTAT.

About the analysis

  • Data: PATSTAT Global, Autumn 2025 edition (European Patent Office): applications, applicants and inventors, technology fields and INPADOC legal events (renewals, lapses and ownership transfers).
  • Sample: granted invention patents filed 2000–2005 at the patent offices of China, Korea, Brazil, the United States, Japan, Germany and the United Kingdom. Owner country is imputed from patent families and first filings where missing.
  • Method: linear probability models of renewal at year ten with office × year and office × technology field fixed effects, office-specific citation effects and owner type, comparing domestic and foreign owners within offices (difference-in-differences across offices); standard errors clustered by owner. Sales: landmark design at patent ages 4–8.
  • Related: Pakes, A. (1986), "Patents as Options," Econometrica; Kogan, L., D. Papanikolaou, A. Seru and N. Stoffman (2017), "Technological Innovation, Resource Allocation, and Growth," Quarterly Journal of Economics.
