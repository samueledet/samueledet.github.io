The Invention, Not the Inventor: Patent Value in Emerging Markets

Samuel Edet · October 2026 · early results from ongoing research · revised

Firms in emerging markets drop their patents sooner than firms in rich economies. Is that because the inventions are worth less, or because the firms cannot afford to turn them into products? Early evidence from 2.6 million firm and university patents points to the invention. Once technology quality is held fixed, emerging-market firms of every size keep their patents at least as long as comparable firms in rich economies. Universities are the exception.

The question

A patent is an option: the right, for up to 20 years, to turn an invention into a product without being copied. Owners pay a renewal fee every year to keep it, and they stop paying when the patent is no longer worth the fee. Renewals therefore reveal value, for private firms as well as listed companies.

If emerging-market patents are dropped early, there are two possible reasons. The underlying technologies may simply be less valuable. Or good inventions may never realise their value because their owners lack the finance, partners or markets to commercialise them. The two call for different policies: research and skills in the first case, finance and technology markets in the second.

How the test works

The analysis uses PATSTAT, the worldwide patent database of the European Patent Office. It follows every patent granted to a firm or a university, filed in 2000–2005 at the patent offices of China, Korea and Brazil, and of the United States, Japan, Germany and the United Kingdom for comparison: 2.6 million patents. The outcome is whether the owner still renews the patent eight years after it was granted. This note covers firms and universities; patents held by individual inventors are left out.

Comparisons are made within the same patent office, where everyone pays the same fees under the same law: domestic owners against foreign owners. The question is whether domestic owners in emerging-market offices fall further behind than domestic owners in rich-economy offices. Technology quality is held fixed using forward citations, technology field and owner type.

Why count from grant: renewal fees only start once a patent is granted, and domestic patents in China and Korea are granted about two years earlier than foreign ones. Counting from the filing date, they would have faced two more renewal decisions by any given age, which inflates their apparent drop-out.

The gap is the technology

Before any adjustment, the gap is real. In China, domestic firms are 13 percentage points more likely than foreign owners to have dropped a patent within eight years of grant, and across emerging-market offices the extra gap is 11 points. But foreign-owned patents are a selected group: inventions judged valuable enough to protect abroad, with five to six times the citations in China and Korea. Once citations, technology field and owner type are accounted for, the gap disappears entirely; at equal quality, emerging-market firms keep their patents slightly longer (by 4 points).

The result holds country by country. Small Chinese firms keep their patents 4 points longer than comparable small firms in rich economies; small Korean firms are level (+1.7, not significant). Brazilian firms hold too few patents in this period to estimate separately. Adding further quality measures, such as citations from examiners showing that a patent blocks later inventions, changes these figures by less than a point.

Universities are the one group that falls short. Emerging-market universities drop their patents 11 points more often than comparable universities in rich economies. Why is not yet clear; one candidate is policies that rewarded universities for filing patents rather than for licensing them.

Where owners still matter

The owner is not irrelevant. Small firms everywhere, rich economies included, keep patents for less time than large firms with patents of the same quality, a sign that the capacity to use an invention matters for small firms in general. And there is one place where emerging-market small firms differ: when they sell a patent, it gains more. A patent sold by a small emerging-market firm is 14 points more likely to still be renewed five years later than a comparable unsold one; for small firms in rich economies the gain is 5 points. This holds when quality is measured only with citations received before the sale.

In China, licensing a patent to a partner raises its survival about as much as selling it (13 points), and does so equally for small domestic firms and foreign owners. Other checks find no sign of the frictions often blamed. When small firms patent the same invention at home and abroad, they keep the home patent longer than larger firms do, in China and Korea as in Japan and the US, so weak protection or a difficult home market is not the problem. Their patents were not dropped more often during credit crunches in China, Korea and Brazil. And when China and Korea cut renewal fees for small firms, renewals moved only a little: in China, an 85% fee discount lowered small firms' yearly drop-out rate from about 4.7% to 4.1%, and Korea's discounts had no measurable effect. Small US firms also respond only weakly to fee changes. Cash at renewal time is not what decides whether a patent is kept.

What the patents are worth

Renewal decisions can be turned into money. If an owner keeps paying a fee, the patent must be worth at least that much to them; if they stop, it is worth less. Fitting a standard model of these decisions to each patent's actual fees gives the value of a patent at grant. Domestic owners value their patents more than foreign owners do in the same office, as expected, since the home market is where they sell. The question is whether that home premium is smaller in emerging markets. It is not: at equal quality, small Chinese and Korean firms' patents are worth about twice those of small foreign firms, within the range seen in rich economies. Chinese universities are again the exception, at well under half.

The same holds patent by patent. Valuing each patent from its own renewal history, without telling the model who owns it, small domestic firms' patents in China and Korea are worth about 20–30% more than small foreign firms' patents with the same fees, citations and technology, much as in the United States and Germany. Chinese universities' patents are worth less than half as much as foreign universities' patents.

What it means, if it holds

  • Development finance and investors: renewal behaviour of emerging-market firms tracks the quality of their technology much as it does in rich economies. There is little sign of valuable inventions stranded by lack of finance, and short-term credit or fee relief is unlikely to change which patents are kept. Quality signals, such as how often a patent is cited, are a reasonable guide to the value of these firms' patent portfolios.
  • Policymakers: the main lever is the quality of the technology, through research, skills and links with frontier firms. Markets for technology help at the margin: small firms' patents gain from being sold or licensed to owners who can use them. University patents are the weak spot: they are dropped sooner than comparable ones in rich economies, which makes how universities are rewarded for patenting worth a closer look.
  • Researchers: survival should be counted from grant, not from filing. Offices grant domestic and foreign patents at different speeds, and counting from filing can create an emerging-market gap that is mostly timing.

These are early results. The dollar levels of patent values depend on the model and should be read as rough; the comparisons between owners are more robust. Citations do not capture every aspect of quality. Patents held by individual inventors are excluded, and Brazil is too thin to estimate on its own. India, Mexico and South Africa cannot yet be studied this way because their renewal records are not in PATSTAT.

About the analysis

  • Data: PATSTAT Global, Autumn 2025 edition (European Patent Office): applications, applicants and inventors, publications, citations, technology fields and INPADOC legal events (renewals, lapses, ownership transfers and licences). Renewal fees compiled from official fee schedules of the seven offices.
  • Sample: granted invention patents of firms and universities, filed 2000–2005 at the patent offices of China, Korea, Brazil, the United States, Japan, Germany and the United Kingdom. Owner country is imputed from patent families and first filings where missing.
  • Method: linear probability models of renewal eight years after grant with office × year and office × technology field fixed effects, office-specific citation effects and owner type, comparing domestic and foreign owners within offices (difference-in-differences across offices); standard errors clustered by owner. Sales and licences: landmark design at patent ages 4–8. Same invention: patent-family fixed effects. Fees: renewal decisions before and after fee changes for eligible and ineligible owners. Values: renewal model (Schankerman and Pakes, 1986) estimated by maximum likelihood on each patent's fee path, with lognormal initial returns by owner group and citations and a 10% discount rate; fees in 2024 dollars (deflated by each country's consumer prices, converted at purchasing-power parity). A version with learning (Pakes, 1986), estimated by simulated maximum likelihood, gives the rate at which returns decline (15% a year, from German and UK fees) and a value for each patent from its renewal history.
  • Related: Pakes, A. (1986), "Patents as Options," Econometrica; Schankerman, M. and A. Pakes (1986), "Estimates of the Value of Patent Rights in European Countries during the Post-1950 Period," Economic Journal; Kogan, L., D. Papanikolaou, A. Seru and N. Stoffman (2017), "Technological Innovation, Resource Allocation, and Growth," Quarterly Journal of Economics.
