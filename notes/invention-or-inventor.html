The Invention, Not the Inventor: Owner Constraints and Patent Value in Emerging Markets

Samuel Edet · November 2026 · results from ongoing research · revised

Firms in emerging markets drop their patents sooner than firms in rich economies. Is that because the inventions are worth less, or because the firms cannot turn them into products? Evidence from 3 million firm and university patents in ten economies points to the invention. Once technology quality is held fixed, larger emerging-market firms keep their patents longer than comparable firms in rich economies, and small firms about as long. Two gaps remain, and they point to where policy can help: markets for technology, and what universities do with their patents.

  • 10points more patents dropped within eight years of grant by emerging-market owners, before adjusting for quality
  • +3 to +9points for emerging-market firms at equal quality, small to large, relative to rich-economy firms
  • +11points in five-year survival when a small emerging-market firm sells a patent, against +5 in rich economies
  • $1.1bna year at stake in China from moving patents to owners who can use them; fee relief: almost nothing

The question

A patent is an option: the right, for up to 20 years, to turn an invention into a product without being copied. Owners pay a renewal fee every year to keep it, and they stop paying when the patent is no longer worth the fee. Renewals therefore reveal value, for private firms as well as listed companies.

If emerging-market patents are dropped early, there are two possible reasons. The underlying technologies may simply be less valuable. Or good inventions may never realise their value because their owners lack the finance, partners or markets to commercialise them. The two call for different policies: research and skills in the first case, finance and technology markets in the second.

How the test works

The analysis uses PATSTAT, the worldwide patent database of the European Patent Office. Only a few emerging economies record renewals and lapses there well enough to study: China, Korea, Russia, Taiwan and the Czech Republic. They are compared with the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France. The sample is every patent granted to a firm or a university and filed in 2000–2005 at these ten offices: 3 million patents. The outcome is whether the owner still renews the patent eight years after it was granted, since renewal fees start at grant. Firm size is the number of inventions a firm filed worldwide in 2000–2005: small firms have 1–9, mid-sized firms 10–99 and large firms 100 or more. Patents held by individual inventors are left out.

Comparisons are made within the same patent office, where everyone pays the same fees under the same law: domestic owners against foreign owners. The question is whether domestic owners in emerging-market offices fall further behind than domestic owners in rich-economy offices. Technology quality is held fixed using forward citations, technology field and owner type.

The gap is the technology

Before any adjustment, the gap is real. In China, domestic firms are 13 percentage points more likely than foreign owners to have dropped a patent within eight years of grant, and across emerging-market offices the extra gap is 10 points. But foreign-owned patents are a selected group: inventions judged valuable enough to protect abroad, with five to six times the citations in China and Korea. Once citations, technology field and owner type are accounted for, the gap disappears: at equal quality, emerging-market owners keep their patents slightly longer overall (by 4 points).

Two checks qualify the small-firm result. With finer quality measures, such as citations from examiners showing that a patent blocks later inventions, small emerging-market firms come out level with small firms in rich economies. And the benchmark matters: Japanese small firms keep their home patents far longer than foreign owners do in Japan, and when China and the US are left out, so that Japan dominates the comparison, small firms in Korea, Russia, Taiwan and the Czech Republic come out 4 points behind. Larger firms stay ahead in every version.

Country by country, Chinese and Korean firms of every size keep their patents at least as long as comparable firms in rich economies, and Czech firms look similar, though the sample is small. The exceptions are small firms in Russia (4 points behind) and, more weakly, Taiwan (2 points). Universities fall short in China and Russia.

Where owners still matter

The owner is not irrelevant. Small firms everywhere, rich economies included, keep patents for less time than large firms with patents of the same quality, a sign that the capacity to use an invention matters for small firms in general. And there is one place where emerging-market owners differ: when they sell a patent, it gains more. A patent sold by a small emerging-market firm is 11 points more likely to still be renewed five years later than a comparable unsold one; for small firms in rich economies the gain is 5 points. The gap is far larger for universities: a patent sold by a Chinese university survives 45 points longer, while in rich economies a sale does not raise the survival of university patents. This holds when quality is measured only with citations received before the sale.

Other checks find no sign of the frictions often blamed on firms. When firms patent the same invention at home and abroad, emerging-market owners keep the home patent relatively longer than rich-economy owners do, so weak protection or a difficult home market is not the problem. Their patents were not dropped more often during credit crunches. And when China cut renewal fees for small firms by 85%, renewals moved only a little: small firms' yearly drop-out rate fell from about 3.6% to 3.3%. Small US firms also respond only weakly to fee changes, and the discounts in Korea and France show no measurable effect. Cash at renewal time is not what decides whether a patent is kept.

What the patents are worth

Renewal decisions can be turned into money. If an owner keeps paying a fee, the patent must be worth at least that much to them; if they stop, it is worth less. Fitting a standard model of these decisions to each patent's actual fees, compiled from the official fee schedules of all ten offices, gives the value of a patent at grant, and valuing each patent from its own renewal history means the owner's identity plays no role. Domestic owners value their patents more than foreign owners do in the same office, as expected, since the home market is where they sell. The question is whether that home premium is smaller in emerging markets.

For all owners together, and for mid-sized and large firms, it is not: the premium is about 40 log points (roughly 50%) in both groups of offices. For small firms it is smaller in emerging markets on average, but again Japan sets the bar; office by office, small firms' premium is 20 log points in China and 49 in Korea, against 23 in the United States and 20 in Germany. Chinese universities are again the exception: their patents are worth less than half as much as foreign universities' patents. The values barely change if every small firm is assumed to pay the discounted fees China and Korea offer.

Russia is left out: domestic owners there paid very low fees yet dropped many patents, and the model cannot tell low value from lapses unrelated to the fee.

What is at stake

The model can also price policies. Each patent is replayed on its own fee schedule with one thing changed, and the result is the change in the returns owners earn from a year's worth of patents. Fee savings are left out, because they are a transfer from the patent office rather than new value. China and Korea are the two emerging-market offices with small-firm fee discounts. Korea records no sales of granted patents, so markets for technology are priced for China only.

A buyer's capacity is set so that the model reproduces the extra survival of patents sold by emerging-market owners of each type; it is applied to every patent of that type, so these are upper bounds. Large firms gain nothing: their sold patents do no better than in rich economies. The two university rows are different routes to the same lost value and should not be added. China's markets-for-technology figures together are about 1.2% of its national R&D spending each year.

Fees are small relative to what patents are worth, so cutting them changes almost no decisions. Helping patents reach owners who can use them is worth over a billion dollars a year in China, and improving what Chinese universities do with their patents, by licensing them to firms or patenting more selectively, is worth hundreds of millions.

What it means, if it holds

  • Development finance and investors: renewal behaviour of emerging-market firms tracks the quality of their technology much as it does in rich economies. There is little sign of valuable inventions stranded by lack of finance, and short-term credit or fee relief is unlikely to change which patents are kept. Quality signals, such as how often a patent is cited, are a reasonable guide to the value of these firms' patent portfolios.
  • Policymakers: the main lever for firms is the quality of the technology, through research, skills and links with frontier firms. Beyond that, the money is in markets for technology, such as registries, licensing intermediaries and secure transfer of title, which help patents reach owners who can use them. For universities, technology-transfer offices and licensing to firms may recover much of the value now lost. Renewal-fee discounts buy very little.

These are results from ongoing research. The dollar levels of patent values depend on the model and should be read as rough; the comparisons between owners are more robust, and the policy figures measure private returns to owners, not the wider value of the knowledge patents spread. Citations do not capture every aspect of quality. Patents held by individual inventors are excluded. Brazil and Hungary record renewals but have too few domestic firm patents, and India, Mexico and South Africa cannot be studied this way because their renewal records are not in PATSTAT.

About the analysis

  • Data: PATSTAT Global, Autumn 2025 edition (European Patent Office): applications, applicants and inventors, publications, citations, technology fields and INPADOC legal events (renewals, lapses, ownership transfers and licences). Renewal fees compiled from official fee schedules of the ten offices, 2000–2025.
  • Sample: granted invention patents of firms and universities, filed 2000–2005 at the patent offices of China, Korea, Russia, Taiwan, the Czech Republic, the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and France. Owner country is imputed from patent families and first filings where missing. Firm size: inventions (patent families) filed worldwide by the firm in 2000–2005.
  • Method: linear probability models of renewal eight years after grant with office × year and office × technology field fixed effects, office-specific citation effects and owner type, comparing domestic and foreign owners within offices (difference-in-differences across offices); standard errors clustered by owner. Sales: landmark design 1–5 years after grant. Same invention: patent-family fixed effects. Fees: renewal decisions before and after fee changes for eligible and ineligible owners. Values: renewal model (Schankerman and Pakes, 1986) estimated by maximum likelihood on each patent's fee path, with lognormal initial returns by owner group and citations and a 10% discount rate; fees in 2024 dollars (deflated by each country's consumer prices, converted at purchasing-power parity). A version with learning (Pakes, 1986), estimated by simulated maximum likelihood, gives the rate at which returns decline (15% a year, from German, UK and French fees) and a value for each patent from its renewal history. Policy figures: the same model, each patent simulated on its own fee path to expiry.
  • Related: Pakes, A. (1986), "Patents as Options," Econometrica; Schankerman, M. and A. Pakes (1986), "Estimates of the Value of Patent Rights in European Countries during the Post-1950 Period," Economic Journal; Kogan, L., D. Papanikolaou, A. Seru and N. Stoffman (2017), "Technological Innovation, Resource Allocation, and Growth," Quarterly Journal of Economics.
