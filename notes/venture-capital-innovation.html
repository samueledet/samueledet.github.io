Scale, not talent: how venture capital drives innovation

Samuel Edet · April 2026 · based on firm-level evidence from 71 countries

Venture capital does more than pick companies that were going to innovate anyway. It causes them to innovate, and it does so mainly by letting them grow, not by changing who they hire.

  • +8.6%more patent families a year after a firm's first VC deal
  • 77%of VC-backed firms had never filed a patent before their first deal
  • +42%growth in employment after VC, with no change in the technical share of staff
  • 82%of the innovation effect runs through that growth in headcount

The question

Do venture investors create innovation, or do they simply spot firms that were already on their way? The answer matters for development finance. If VC only selects winners, public money that crowds in VC mostly shifts returns to investors. If VC causes innovation, it creates new knowledge that spills over to others, and that is a case for blended finance and supportive regulation.

The study links 35,508 VC-backed firms in 71 countries to their patent records (PATSTAT) and workforce data (Revelio Labs). Each firm is compared with similar firms that had not yet raised VC at the same point in time, using a staggered difference-in-differences design (Callaway and Sant'Anna, 2021). Before the deal, the two groups follow the same innovation path. After it, they diverge.

VC causes innovation

A first VC investment raises a firm's yearly patent filings by 8.6%. The effect builds over three to five years, in line with the lag between research and a patent filing. It holds in nine of ten robustness checks, including dropping the US or China and using alternative estimators.

Most of the effect comes from firms that start patenting for the first time: VC raises the chance that a firm files any patent in a given year by 7.1 percentage points. The patents are also better. They are cited more and score higher on measures of generality and originality, so they are not just defensive filings.

The mechanism: scale first, governance second, not talent

There are three common explanations of how VC spurs innovation:

  • Talent: VC attracts better scientists and engineers.
  • Scale: VC lets firms hire more people and expand research capacity.
  • Governance: VC brings professional management, such as a chief financial officer (CFO) and changes to the founding team.

The data clearly favour scale. After VC, employment rises by about 42%. The share of technical workers, average seniority and the technical mix of new hires do not change. The chance of having a CFO rises by about 6 points and founder departures by about 7 points, so governance does change. But these changes add little to patenting once growth is accounted for.

This is good news for countries with thin technical labour markets. They do not need a deep pool of scientists before VC can raise innovation. What they need is room for firms to grow, and a working patent system.

Where VC works best

Later stages. The effect rises steadily with the stage of the deal. Late-stage rounds have about eight times the effect of seed rounds, because they fund firms that have already shown they can produce intellectual property.

East Asia and Pacific. The effect is largest in East Asia and Pacific, about twice that in North America or Europe. VC there coincided with a state-driven expansion of patenting in China and South Korea.

The emerging-market premium is a China story. Across emerging markets and developing economies, VC's effect looks larger than in advanced economies (+13.4% against +8.4%). But China drives all of it. Without China, the emerging-market effect falls to +2.3%, which cannot be distinguished from zero. China combines large VC markets, strong incentives to patent and integrated manufacturing. Elsewhere in the developing world, the limit is the wider ecosystem rather than the supply of VC.

What does a VC-induced patent cost?

Combining the effect with typical first-round deal sizes gives about $7 million of first-round capital per additional patent family worldwide. It is about $15 million in emerging markets, where first cheques are larger. But emerging-market founders give up less equity per round (14–17%, against 25–27% in advanced economies). So in terms of ownership given up, an extra patent costs founders there roughly half as much.

What it means

  • Development finance institutions: VC's innovation payoff does not depend on a deep local supply of scientists, so catalytic finance can work in markets with thin technical labour pools.
  • Fund managers: the innovation payoff is concentrated in later rounds, so growth-stage capital does the most to create intellectual property.
  • Policymakers: a credible, accessible patent system appears to matter more than subsidies for science and engineering education. Investing in patent-office capacity and firm-level IP know-how may do more than R&D tax credits.

Patents are an imperfect measure of innovation. They miss unpatented process and business-model innovation, and the propensity to patent varies by sector and country. Workforce data cover a subset of firms, tilted toward larger and more visible companies. The estimates are average effects.

About the research

  • Edet, S. (2026). "Venture Capital, Scale, and the Reorientation of Innovation: Firm-Level Evidence from 71 Countries." SSRN working paper. ssrn.com/abstract=7645558
  • Method: Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) staggered difference-in-differences with doubly-robust inference; 23,850 firms first financed in 2007–2019 compared with firms financed later.
  • Data: PATSTAT (European Patent Office) for patents, Revelio Labs for workforce, and Crunchbase and PitchBook for deals.
