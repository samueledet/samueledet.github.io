Forecast the risk, not the return: what neural networks can predict in stock markets

Samuel Edet · January 2026 · revisiting my 2017 paper on recurrent neural networks and the S&P 500, now tested in 11 markets, 2013–2025

In 2017 I asked whether recurrent neural networks could predict the daily direction of the S&P 500. Nine years later, with a stricter test and 11 markets, the answer is no. The same networks are, however, the best of the models tested at forecasting how volatile markets will be, and that forecast is useful: it cut the worst losses in 10 of 11 markets.

  • 0 of 11markets where the network beat "always predict up" by more than 0.3 percentage points
  • 10 of 11markets where the network was the most accurate volatility forecaster tested
  • 21%median reduction in volatility forecast error against last month's volatility
  • 10 of 11markets where volatility targeting reduced the maximum drawdown

Revisiting the 2017 result

The 2017 paper reported that recurrent networks predicted the S&P 500's daily direction about 74% of the time. A review of the code found that this came from how the prediction target was built, not from the networks. The label was whether today's return beat yesterday's, and yesterday's return was also an input, so the label was partly predictable even from pure noise. On clean data, a simple logistic regression matches the 74%, and random numbers reach 65%. When the target is redefined as whether the market rises tomorrow, and the model is tested on later years it has not seen, accuracy falls to about 49–54%.

That raised a better question: if not the direction of returns, what can these networks forecast?

How the test works

The study covers the main stock index of five developed markets (United States, Japan, United Kingdom, Germany, Australia) and six emerging markets (Brazil, Mexico, India, China, South Africa, Indonesia). For each year from 2013 to 2025, every model is trained only on data from earlier years and then forecasts each day of that year, about 3,200 trading days per market. An LSTM network, using the past month of returns and volatility, competes with simple benchmarks on two tasks: will the index rise tomorrow, and how volatile will the next week be?

Direction: no better than "always up"

Stock indices rise on slightly more than half of all days, so always predicting "up" is right 51–55% of the time. The network never did meaningfully better. Its edge ranged from −2.2 to +0.3 percentage points, and a trading rule built on its signals had a lower Sharpe ratio than buy and hold in 9 of 11 markets. A US test over 2020–2025 told the same story: the networks were right 49–50% of the time, against 55% for "always up".

Volatility: where the networks earn their keep

Volatility is persistent. Turbulent weeks follow turbulent weeks, and that structure is something a network can learn. Measured against the simplest forecast, last month's volatility, the LSTM reduced forecast errors by 10% to 30%. It beat the standard HAR model (median 14%) in 10 of 11 markets. A GARCH model, the textbook tool, did no better than last month's volatility on this test.

Putting the forecast to work: volatility targeting

A volatility forecast can be used to size positions: hold less of the market when the forecast is high and more when it is low, aiming for a steady 15% annual volatility. Run this way, after trading costs, the strategy cut the largest peak-to-trough loss in 10 of 11 markets, typically by 6 to 16 percentage points. It did not raise risk-adjusted returns: the Sharpe ratio beat buy and hold only in India, so the benefit is a smoother ride, not a higher return.

What it means

  • Investors and asset managers: be sceptical of machine-learning claims to predict daily market direction. Results like the 2017 ones usually come from a flaw in the test design. The test that matters is out of sample, in time order, against "always up".
  • Risk managers: neural networks add real value for volatility forecasting, which feeds Value at Risk, margin and position limits. They beat both the simple benchmark and HAR in most markets.
  • Portfolio construction: volatility targeting with good forecasts is a practical way to limit drawdowns, including in emerging markets with the deepest falls. It manages risk; it is not a source of extra return.

The study uses daily closing prices of price indices in local currency. Networks use only each market's own price history. A separate S&P 500 test with richer inputs (VIX, yields, credit, gold, the dollar and foreign markets) and a one-month horizon raised the Sharpe ratio of a volatility-targeted strategy from 0.80 to 0.90 and halved its worst drawdown, but not significantly. Replicated unchanged in the other 10 markets, it again cut drawdowns everywhere but beat buy and hold's Sharpe ratio only in Brazil and India. Model settings were fixed in advance, not tuned on the test years. Results cover one network design and one period, 2013–2025, which includes the 2020 crash and the 2022 rate shock.

About the analysis

  • Edet, S. (2017). "Recurrent Neural Networks in Forecasting S&P 500 Index." SSRN working paper. ssrn.com/abstract=3001046
  • Method: walk-forward evaluation, 2013–2025, with models retrained each year on all earlier data. LSTM network (one layer, 32 units, 22-day input window) against "always up", logistic regression, last month's volatility, HAR (Corsi, 2009) and GARCH(1,1). Volatility target: 15% a year, maximum exposure 1.5 times.
  • Data: daily closing levels of 11 stock indices from Yahoo Finance, 2005–2025.
