Forecast the risk, not the return: what neural networks can predict in stock markets

Samuel Edet · January 2026 · revisiting my 2017 paper on recurrent neural networks and the S&P 500, now tested in 11 markets, 2013–2025

In 2017 I asked whether recurrent neural networks could predict the daily direction of the S&P 500. Nine years later, with a stricter test, 11 markets and a rich set of market, macro and cross-asset inputs, the answer is still no. The same networks are, however, the best of the models tested at forecasting how volatile markets will be, and that forecast is useful: it cut the worst losses in 10 of 11 markets.

  • 10 of 11markets where no model beat "always predict up" on next month's direction
  • 10 of 11markets where the network was the most accurate volatility forecaster tested
  • 21%median reduction in volatility forecast error against last month's volatility
  • 10 of 11markets where volatility targeting reduced the maximum drawdown

Revisiting the 2017 result

The 2017 paper reported that recurrent networks predicted the S&P 500's daily direction about 74% of the time. A review of the code found that this came from how the prediction target was built, not from the networks. The label was whether today's return beat yesterday's, and yesterday's return was also an input, so the label was partly predictable even from pure noise. On clean data, a simple logistic regression matches the 74%, and random numbers reach 65%. When the target is redefined as whether the market rises tomorrow, and the model is tested on later years it has not seen, accuracy falls to about 49–54%.

That raised a better question: if not the direction of returns, what can these networks forecast?

How the test works

The study covers the main stock index of five developed markets (United States, Japan, United Kingdom, Germany, Australia) and six emerging markets (Brazil, Mexico, India, China, South Africa, Indonesia). For each year from 2013 to 2025, every model is trained only on data from earlier years and then forecasts each day of that year, about 3,200 trading days per market. All settings were fixed in advance.

  • Direction: will the index be higher in a month (20 trading days)? The models see 26 to 28 inputs: the market's own momentum over one week to one year, its trend and drawdown, its recent volatility, the VIX and the variance risk premium, US yields and the term spread, Treasury, corporate bond, gold and dollar returns, and the latest US close (for the US itself, same-day European and Asian returns). Four models compete: logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, an LSTM network and the average of the three.
  • Volatility: how volatile will the next week be? An LSTM using the past month of the market's own returns competes with last month's volatility, the HAR model and GARCH.

Direction: no better than "always up"

Markets rise in most months, so always predicting "up" over the next month was right 54–70% of the time. That is the bar a model has to clear, and with all inputs and all four models, the best of them failed to clear it in 10 of 11 markets. Only in Brazil did the models do better, and one success among 11 markets is about what chance alone would produce.

Trading on the signals tells the same story. A rule that stays fully invested when the LSTM expects a rise and half invested otherwise had a higher Sharpe ratio than buy and hold in 5 of 11 markets, by small margins, and the difference was not statistically significant in any of them. Next-day direction, tested separately, was no more predictable: on 2020–2025 US data the networks were right 49–50% of the time, against 55% for "always up".

Volatility: where the networks earn their keep

Volatility is persistent. Turbulent weeks follow turbulent weeks, and that structure is something a network can learn. Measured against the simplest forecast, last month's volatility, the LSTM reduced forecast errors by 10% to 30%. It beat the standard HAR model (median 14%) in 10 of 11 markets. A GARCH model, the textbook tool, did no better than last month's volatility on this test.

Putting the forecast to work: volatility targeting

A volatility forecast can be used to size positions: hold less of the market when the forecast is high and more when it is low, aiming for a steady 15% annual volatility. Run this way, after trading costs, the strategy cut the largest peak-to-trough loss in 10 of 11 markets, typically by 6 to 16 percentage points. It did not raise risk-adjusted returns: the Sharpe ratio beat buy and hold only in India, so the benefit is a smoother ride, not a higher return. Combining volatility targeting with the direction signals did not change this: it again cut drawdowns in every market, but beat buy and hold's Sharpe ratio only in the US, Brazil and India, and significantly only in Brazil.

What it means

  • Investors and asset managers: be sceptical of machine-learning claims to predict market direction, even with rich inputs. Results like the 2017 ones usually come from a flaw in the test design. The test that matters is out of sample, in time order, against "always up".
  • Risk managers: neural networks add real value for volatility forecasting, which feeds Value at Risk, margin and position limits. They beat both the simple benchmark and HAR in most markets.
  • Portfolio construction: volatility targeting with good forecasts is a practical way to limit drawdowns, including in emerging markets with the deepest falls. It manages risk; it is not a source of extra return.

The study uses daily closing prices of price indices in local currency, so dividends are excluded. Model settings were fixed in advance, not tuned on the test years; other designs or inputs, such as company fundamentals, analyst revisions or fund flows, might do better. Results cover one period, 2013–2025, which includes the 2020 crash and the 2022 rate shock, both of which favour volatility management.

About the analysis

  • Edet, S. (2017). "Recurrent Neural Networks in Forecasting S&P 500 Index." SSRN working paper. ssrn.com/abstract=3001046
  • Method: walk-forward evaluation, 2013–2025, with models retrained each year on all earlier data; training targets that overlap the test year are dropped. Direction: one-month horizon, logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees and an LSTM (60-day input window) and their average, against "always up". Volatility: LSTM (22-day window) against last month's volatility, HAR (Corsi, 2009) and GARCH(1,1). Volatility target: 15% a year, maximum exposure 1.5 times, 5 basis points cost per unit traded. Significance: block bootstrap of the Sharpe ratio difference.
  • Data: daily closing levels of 11 stock indices, plus the VIX, US Treasury yields, the dollar index and Treasury, corporate bond and gold ETFs, from Yahoo Finance, 2004–2025.
