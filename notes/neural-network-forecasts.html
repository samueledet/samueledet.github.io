Forecast the risk, not the return: what machine learning can predict in stock markets

Samuel Edet · January 2026 · revisiting my 2017 paper on recurrent neural networks and the S&P 500, now tested in 11 markets, 2013–2025

In 2017 I asked whether recurrent neural networks could predict the direction of the S&P 500. Nine years later, with a stricter test, 11 markets and a rich set of market, macro and cross-asset inputs, the answer is still no, for neural networks and for every other model tested. Volatility is different: the same inputs cut volatility forecast errors by a median 19%, though a simple combination of models beats the network on its own. Used to size positions, those forecasts reduced the worst losses in most markets, but did not reliably beat buy and hold.

  • 10 of 11markets where no model beat "always predict up" on next month's direction
  • 19%median cut in volatility forecast error against last month's volatility
  • 10 of 11markets where the combined forecast beat the standard HAR volatility model
  • 9 of 11markets where volatility targeting reduced the maximum drawdown

Revisiting the 2017 result

The 2017 paper reported that recurrent networks predicted the S&P 500's daily direction about 74% of the time. A review of the code found that this came from how the prediction target was built, not from the networks. The label was whether today's return beat yesterday's, and yesterday's return was also an input, so the label was partly predictable even from pure noise. On clean data, a simple logistic regression matches the 74%, and random numbers reach 65%.

That raised two better questions. With a sound test and much richer information, can machine learning predict where markets are heading? And if not the direction of returns, what can it forecast?

How the test works

The study covers the main stock index of five developed markets (United States, Japan, United Kingdom, Germany, Australia) and six emerging markets (Brazil, Mexico, India, China, South Africa, Indonesia). For each year from 2013 to 2025, every model is trained only on data from earlier years and then forecasts each day of that year, about 3,200 trading days per market. All settings were fixed in advance.

Every model sees the same 26 to 28 inputs: the market's own momentum over one week to one year, its trend and drawdown, its recent volatility, the VIX and the variance risk premium, US yields and the term spread, Treasury, corporate bond, gold and dollar returns, and the latest US close (for the US itself, same-day European and Asian returns). Each input is taken as it was known at the market's close.

  • Direction: will the index be higher in a month (20 trading days)? Logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, an LSTM network and the average of the three, against "always up".
  • Volatility: how volatile will the next week be? A linear model, gradient-boosted trees, an LSTM and their average, against three standard benchmarks: last month's volatility, the HAR model and GARCH.

Direction: no better than "always up"

Markets rise in most months, so always predicting "up" over the next month was right 54–70% of the time. That is the bar a model has to clear, and with all inputs and all four models, the best of them failed to clear it in 10 of 11 markets. Only in Brazil did the models do better, and one success among 11 markets is about what chance alone would produce.

Trading on the signals tells the same story. A rule that stays fully invested when the LSTM expects a rise and half invested otherwise had a higher Sharpe ratio than buy and hold in 5 of 11 markets, by small margins, and the difference was not statistically significant in any of them.

Volatility: forecastable, but the network is not the best tool

Volatility is persistent. Turbulent weeks follow turbulent weeks, and option prices, captured by the VIX, carry the market's own view of the risk ahead. With the rich inputs, a simple average of the linear model, the trees and the LSTM reduced the squared error of next-week volatility forecasts by a median 19% against last month's volatility, and beat the standard HAR model in 10 of 11 markets.

The network on its own did less well. Its median gain was 13%, below HAR's 14%, and it beat HAR in only 5 of 11 markets. A plain linear model using the same inputs did better (17%). With many inputs and a limited history, the network overfits; averaging it with simpler models cancels part of that error. GARCH, the textbook tool, did slightly worse than last month's volatility.

Putting the forecast to work: volatility targeting

A volatility forecast can be used to size positions: hold less of the market when the forecast is high and more when it is low, aiming for a steady 15% annual volatility. Run this way with the combined forecast, after trading costs, the strategy cut the largest peak-to-trough loss in 9 of 11 markets, typically by 6 to 16 percentage points. In the UK and Mexico the drawdown was larger. It did not reliably raise risk-adjusted returns: the Sharpe ratio was higher than buy and hold's in 3 of 11 markets (United States, India and China), and not significantly in any. Adding the direction signals on top did not change this. The benefit is a smoother ride in most markets, not a higher return.

What it means

  • Investors and asset managers: be sceptical of machine-learning claims to predict market direction, even with rich inputs. Results like the 2017 ones usually come from a flaw in the test design. The test that matters is out of sample, in time order, against "always up".
  • Risk managers: volatility forecasts feed Value at Risk, margin and position limits, and better inputs, the VIX above all, improve them clearly. A simple combination of models is more reliable than any single one; a neural network on its own adds little over a linear model with good inputs.
  • Portfolio construction: volatility targeting is a practical way to limit drawdowns, including in emerging markets with the deepest falls, but it does not work everywhere and is not a source of extra return. Its value is in managing risk.

The study uses daily closing prices of price indices in local currency, so dividends are excluded. Model settings were fixed in advance, not tuned on the test years; other designs or inputs, such as local option-implied volatility, company fundamentals or fund flows, might do better. Results cover one period, 2013–2025, which includes the 2020 crash and the 2022 rate shock.

About the analysis

  • Edet, S. (2017). "Recurrent Neural Networks in Forecasting S&P 500 Index." SSRN working paper. ssrn.com/abstract=3001046
  • Method: walk-forward evaluation, 2013–2025, with models retrained each year on all earlier data; training targets that overlap the test year are dropped. Direction: one-month horizon; logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, an LSTM (60-day input window) and their average, against "always up". Volatility: next-week log realised variance; linear regression, gradient-boosted trees, an LSTM and their average, against last month's volatility, HAR (Corsi, 2009) and GARCH(1,1). Volatility target: 15% a year, maximum exposure 1.5 times, 5 basis points cost per unit traded. Significance: block bootstrap of the Sharpe ratio difference.
  • Data: daily closing levels of 11 stock indices, plus the VIX, US Treasury yields, the dollar index and Treasury, corporate bond and gold ETFs, from Yahoo Finance, 2004–2025.
